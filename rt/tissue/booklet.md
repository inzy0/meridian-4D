# The Tendon Booklet — Structure, Adaptation, Loading, Recovery and Injury Prediction

Nov 24, 2026 · @Inzamam shaikh

## How to use this booklet

A tendon is a living rope that adapts to load slowly, recovers in hours to days, and fails when load outruns adaptation. Every chapter builds on that one idea. Chapters 1–3 explain what a tendon is and how it adapts. Chapters 4–7 cover dosing, recovery and risk. Chapters 8–11 turn this into assessment, health, body-region rehab and a practical playbook. Chapters 12–17 cover same-day loading, supporting structures, tears and healing, treatments and technology, measurement and new ideas. Chapter 18 lists what is still unknown.

Each claim carries an evidence grade. Grades describe the *type* of evidence, not whether the claim is true.

| Grade | Evidence type | How to treat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Meta-analysis or high-quality RCT in humans | Use as default practice |
| B | Controlled human trial, prospective cohort, tracer/biopsy study | Use, but watch individual response |
| C | Animal or in-vitro (cells, engineered tissue) | Mechanism only; test before relying on it |
| D | Expert model, consensus or clinical reasoning | A starting rule, open to change |

**Words used throughout.** *Load* is any force the tendon carries. *Strain* is how much the tendon stretches as a percentage of its length. *Stiffness* is the force needed per millimetre of stretch. *Capacity* is the load a tendon can tolerate without symptoms or damage. *Tendinopathy* is the clinical syndrome of load-related tendon pain and lost function. It is not the same as an abnormal scan.

## Chapter 1 — What a tendon is

**A tendon is a dense, water-rich collagen cable that transmits muscle force to bone and stores elastic energy.** It is not inert. It contains living cells that sense load and remodel the matrix around them, slowly.

### 1.1 Composition

By wet weight, a tendon is mostly water. [Reviews give](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6917864) 55–70% water and 30–45% dry mass, almost all of it extracellular matrix. Of that dry mass, 60–85% is collagen. About 95% of the collagen is type I, with small amounts of types III, V, XII and XIV ([Thorpe et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3721456)).

The rest of the matrix is small but important. Elastin makes up about 1–2% of dry mass ([Kannus 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11085557/)). Proteoglycans such as decorin and biglycan coat the collagen fibrils. In compressed regions they bind water and resist squeezing ([Thorpe & Screen review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507431)). Glycoproteins such as lubricin, COMP and tenascin-C help fibres slide and stay organized.

| Component | Share | Job | What happens when it changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water | 55–70% of wet mass | Gives viscoelastic behaviour; carries nutrients | Pushed out under load and re-absorbed over hours (Ch. 4) |
| Collagen I | \~95% of collagen; 60–85% of dry mass | Carries tensile load | Disorganized and thinner fibrils in tendinopathy |
| Collagen III | Small | Fast, weaker repair collagen | Rises in healing and pathology; mechanically inferior |
| Proteoglycans (decorin, biglycan, aggrecan) | 1–5% of dry mass | Space fibrils; resist compression | Aggrecan rises in compressed or diseased tendon, which swells |
| Elastin | 1–2% of dry mass | Recoil; helps fascicles slide | Falls with age |
| Tenocytes | Sparse cells | Sense strain; make and break matrix | Become rounder and more active in reactive tendon |

### 1.2 Hierarchy

Collagen is built in nested levels. Each level is bound by its own sheath, and each level can slide relative to its neighbours.

1. **Collagen molecule** (triple helix, about 300 nm long)
2. **Fibril** (molecules staggered and crosslinked; tens to hundreds of nm wide)
3. **Fibre** (bundles of fibrils)
4. **Fascicle** (fibres wrapped in endotenon; the main load unit)
5. **Tendon** (fascicles wrapped in epitenon, often with a paratenon or sheath)

The matrix *between* fascicles, called the interfascicular matrix, lets fascicles slide. This sliding matters most in energy-storing tendons, which have to stretch a lot ([Thorpe et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3721456)).

### 1.3 The two ends: muscle–tendon junction and enthesis

At the **muscle–tendon junction (MTJ)**, muscle fibres fold into the tendon to increase contact area. This region is compliant and is where most muscle strains occur. At the **enthesis**, the tendon inserts into bone through layers: tendon, uncalcified fibrocartilage, calcified fibrocartilage, then bone. This gradient spreads stress. Insertional pain (Achilles insertion, proximal hamstring, gluteal) often involves **compression** as well as tension, which changes rehab choices (Ch. 10).

### 1.4 Energy-storing vs positional tendons

Tendons fall into two functional classes. **Energy-storing tendons** (Achilles, patellar) work like springs. They stretch a lot, take high loads and fail most often. **Positional tendons** (finger extensors, many upper-limb tendons) are stiffer and mainly place the joint. This is why the same exercise dose cannot be copied from one tendon to another.

| Feature | Energy-storing (Achilles, patellar) | Positional (e.g. digital extensors) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical in-use strain | High | Low |
| Fascicle sliding | High | Low |
| Injury rate | High | Low |
| Change with age | Loses its fatigue resistance | More stable |
| Rehab emphasis | Rate and spring loading late in rehab | Mainly strength and control |

### 1.5 Blood supply and turnover

Tendons get little blood at rest. With exercise, blood flow in and around the tendon rises [three- to seven-fold](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2100210). Even so, carbon-14 bomb-pulse dating shows the core collagen of the adult Achilles is [essentially not replaced in adult life](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3633810). Most adaptation in adults therefore happens at the periphery, in the non-collagen matrix and in crosslinks, not by building a new core.

**Go deeper:** Thorpe & Screen, "Tendon structure and composition" (2016, Adv Exp Med Biol); Magnusson & Kjaer, "The impact of loading, unloading, ageing and injury on the human tendon" (J Physiol 2019).

## Chapter 2 — Mechanical properties

**A tendon's job is to carry very high forces at a controlled stretch.** The key numbers are force (how much load), strain (how much stretch), stiffness (force per stretch), and the margin between everyday strain and failure strain.

### 2.1 How much load tendons carry

The Achilles is the body's largest and strongest tendon. One modelling study of running predicted [peak Achilles forces of 5.3–10.0 times body weight](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7132650/). Komi's in-vivo transducer work estimated a [peak of about 9 kN, or 12.5 times body weight](https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=114289), during running. Other models give about [3.9 times body weight walking and 7.7 times running](https://uk.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/the-achilles-tendon-and-its-pathologies). In a unilateral jump landing the Achilles peaked at [6.68 times body weight](https://canada.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/the-achilles-tendon-and-its-pathologies), versus 0.77 in a squat.

The patellar tendon is loaded even harder per unit area. Estimates reach [up to 17.5 times body weight in weightlifting](https://sma.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Tendons-and-Jumping.-Anatomy-and-Pathomechanics-of-Tendon-Injuries.pdf), with landing forces higher than take-off.

| Task | Achilles force (× body weight) | Source type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | \~0.8 | Musculoskeletal model |
| Walking | \~2–4 | Models and transducers |
| Running | \~5–12.5 | Models (5.3–10); transducer (12.5) |
| Single-leg jump landing | \~6.7 | Musculoskeletal model |
| Hopping | \~3.8 kN absolute | Implanted transducer |

**Practical meaning:** a calf raise with body weight does not come close to running loads. Moment arms explain why: the Achilles has a short lever at the ankle, so internal tendon force is several times the external load. Heavy, loaded calf work is needed to approach sport-level tendon force.

### 2.2 Strain, stiffness and the safety factor

Measured Achilles strain ranges from about [4.3% in walking up to 9.0% in fast running](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7332733). Tendon ultimate strain, the stretch at which it fails, is remarkably constant across people. So if muscle gets stronger but the tendon does not stiffen, the tendon works closer to its limit. This ratio is the **safety factor**, and a falling safety factor is a proposed cause of overuse injury ([Mersmann et al. review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7332733)).

- **Stiffness** (N/mm) depends on size (cross-sectional area) and material (Young's modulus).
- **Young's modulus** (MPa or GPa) is the material's own stiffness. Training changes the modulus more than size ([Bohm 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9)).
- **Toe region:** at low strain (<2%), crimped collagen straightens. **Linear region:** collagen carries load directly. **Failure:** beyond about 8–12% strain in isolated tissue.

### 2.3 Viscoelasticity

Tendons behave partly like springs (elastic) and partly like shock absorbers (viscous). That produces four practical effects:

| Effect | What happens | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate dependence | Faster loading makes the tendon act stiffer | Fast stretch-shortening loads (jumps) feel stiffer than slow lifts |
| Creep | Under a constant load, length slowly increases | Long holds gradually lengthen the tendon |
| Stress relaxation | At a held length, force slowly drops | The basis of isometric analgesia ideas; see Ch. 5 |
| Hysteresis | Some energy is lost as heat each cycle | Repeated cycles warm the tendon; heat may damage cells in extreme cases |

### 2.4 Regional differences

Tendons are not uniform along their length. In rat tibialis anterior, strain near the muscle was [five times greater than mid-tendon or near the bone](https://scilit.com/publications/0eb18aeb2dce6c2ead20cf5cc01ecae9). Human patellar tendon also differs front to back: [anterior fascicles are stronger and stiffer than posterior ones](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3191794). This may explain why patellar tendinopathy usually sits at the posterior, deep proximal tendon.

### 2.5 Athletes have sport-shaped tendons

A comparison of elite groups normalized tendon size to body mass. [Ski jumpers and runners had larger tendons than controls, and water polo players smaller ones](https://doi.org//10.1371/journal.pone.0158441). Normalized stiffness differed only in ski jumpers. Habitual load builds size first; stiffness changes are harder to detect.

**Go deeper:** Arampatzis group papers on strain-based tendon training (Humboldt University Berlin); Maganaris & Paul on in-vivo tendon mechanics.

## Chapter 3 — How tendons adapt

**Tendon adapts through a slow cycle: load → cell signal → breakdown and synthesis → net remodelling over weeks to months.** Breakdown comes first. That is why rest between hard sessions matters more for tendon than for muscle.

### 3.1 Mechanotransduction: how cells "feel" load

Tenocytes sit in rows between collagen fibres. When the tendon stretches, the matrix deforms the cells through integrins and the cytoskeleton. This switches on signalling (FAK, ERK1/2) and gene programs. After exercise, human tendon tissue shows rises in [TGF-β, PGE2, IGF-I and its binding proteins, and IL-6](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/from-mechanical-loading-to-collagen-synthesis-structural-changes-/). IGF-I and procollagen rose similarly [whether loading was concentric, isometric or eccentric](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/from-mechanical-loading-to-collagen-synthesis-structural-changes-/). In other words, the cell responds to **strain magnitude**, not to contraction type.

### 3.2 The collagen timeline after one session

| Time after loading | What happens | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–10 min | Cell signalling peaks, then switches off | Engineered ligament, [Paxton 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3267962) (C) |
| 0–6 h | Cells refractory to new signal | Paxton 2012 (C) |
| 0–\~24 h | Breakdown rises and peaks early; net balance can be negative | [Magnusson 2010](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/the-pathogenesis-of-tendinopathy-balancing-the-response-to-loadin/) (B) |
| \~24 h | Collagen synthesis peaks | Magnusson 2010; [Miller 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1474228) (B) |
| \~72 h | Synthesis still elevated; net formation | Magnusson 2010; [Langberg 1999](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2269656) (B) |

In the [Copenhagen model](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/the-pathogenesis-of-tendinopathy-balancing-the-response-to-loadin/), collagen formation peaks around 24 h after exercise and stays raised for about 3 days, while degradation peaks earlier. [Without enough rest (about 24 h)](https://www.nature.com/nrrheum/journal/v6/n5/full/nrrheum.2010.43.html), net collagen loss may occur and leave the tendon vulnerable.

Langberg's microdialysis study of runners captured this directly. Markers of both synthesis and breakdown [fell right after a 36 km run, then rebounded over the next 3 days, with synthesis rebounding more](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2269656). Over weeks of training, [synthesis and breakdown both rise early; later, synthesis dominates and produces a net gain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2278680).

**Practical rule (B):** schedule heavy tendon loading about 48–72 h apart. Light loading on the days between is fine and probably helpful.

### 3.3 Turnover: what can actually change

The core of the adult Achilles is [not renewed during adult life](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3633810). In tendinopathic tendons, bomb-pulse data show [a substantial renewal had occurred](https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/carbon-14-bomb-pulse-dating-shows-that-tendinopathy-is-preceded-b/). Modelling suggested years of abnormally high turnover before symptoms appeared. The interpretation: pathology is a long, quiet remodelling failure, and pain is a late signal.

So training mainly changes:

1. **Peripheral and newly formed collagen.** Cross-sectional area grows a few percent over 3–4 months.
2. **Material quality (modulus).** Crosslinks, fibril packing and water binding change.
3. **Non-collagen matrix.** Proteoglycans and interfascicular matrix change.

### 3.4 Crosslinks

| Type | Made by | Effect | Raised by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Enzymatic (pyridinoline, HP/LP) | Lysyl oxidase (LOX) | Healthy stiffness and strength | Loading; LOX is inhibited by high estrogen in vitro ([Lee 2015](https://pub-9d851b3285154f429229ea9e0930d687.r2.dev/research-pdfs/estrogen-inhibits-lysyl-oxidase-and-decreases-mechanical-function-in-engineered-ligaments.pdf)) |
| Non-enzymatic (AGEs, pentosidine) | Sugar reacting with collagen | Brittle stiffness; less fascicle sliding | Age, diabetes, high blood glucose |

### 3.5 Adaptation size and speed

A meta-analysis of 27 studies found tendons are [highly responsive to diverse loading regimens](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9). The best results came from [high intensity over more than 12 weeks](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9). Most of the stiffness gain came from [material changes rather than size](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9).

In one controlled trial, 14 weeks of 3-s holds at high strain produced [57% more stiffness, 4.2% more CSA and 51% higher modulus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25267851/).

### 3.6 The muscle–tendon mismatch

Muscle strength can rise within weeks; tendon stiffness takes months. If muscle outpaces tendon, the tendon works at higher strain for the same relative effort and its safety factor falls ([Mersmann review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7332733)). The risk is highest in:

- adolescents during growth spurts;
- athletes starting heavy strength or jump blocks;
- athletes returning from a layoff, because muscle regains faster than tendon.

Targeted high-strain loading can close the gap. In one study, an individualized program [reduced muscle–tendon imbalances and especially stiffened tendons that had high strain at baseline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11519156).

### 3.7 Factors that change the response

| Factor | Effect | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sex | Women have lower resting and post-exercise collagen synthesis, and [habitual training enlarged tendons in men but not women](https://www.pubmed.gov/17696904/) | B |
| Oral contraceptives | High-estrogen OC users showed [a smaller exercise-induced rise in collagen synthesis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2517199) | B |
| Age | Older tendons still adapt: older women showed [mechanosensitivity after 14 weeks of cyclic strain exercise](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/6084/1/1008.full.pdf). Response is slower | B |
| Unloading | Collagen synthesis fell [from 0.052 to 0.010 %/h over 21 days](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2375459); tendon size held | B |
| Nutrition | Collagen peptides plus training gave [larger CSA gains (+11.0% vs +4.7%)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35403756/) but no extra stiffness | B |

**Go deeper:** Magnusson, Langberg & Kjaer, "The pathogenesis of tendinopathy: balancing the response to loading" (Nat Rev Rheumatol 2010); Bohm et al. 2015 meta-analysis (Sports Med Open).

## Chapter 4 — Fatigue, recovery, response and deterioration

**Each loading session leaves the tendon in a temporary state: mechanically recovered within about 24 h, biologically recovered after about 72 h, and structurally changed only over months.** Most tendon problems come from stacking sessions before those clocks finish.

### 4.1 The three recovery clocks

| Clock | What recovers | Typical time | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanical (fluid) | Tendon thickness and water content | Most within \~6 h; full by \~24 h | B |
| Biological (matrix) | Breakdown/synthesis balance turns positive | \~24–72 h | B |
| Structural | Size, modulus, stiffness | 8–12+ weeks | A |
| Symptomatic (tendinopathy) | Pain response to a load test | Next morning (24 h) | B |

### 4.2 Acute mechanical response: the tendon "squeezes out" water

Heavy resistance work causes an [immediate drop of about 15% in Achilles thickness](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3102919). Thickness returned to baseline within 24 h, with most recovery in the first 6 h. The proposed mechanism is collagen realignment and fluid pushed out of the tendon core.

Eccentric loading caused a [larger acute drop than concentric loading at similar impulse (−0.21 vs −0.05)](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/id/eprint/18750), with a recovery time constant of 2.5 h. In both Achilles and patellar tendons, the [transverse stretch ratio was about 0.86 and 0.82](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/48/5/383.full.pdf) right after resistance exercise. The response shrank with age, and recovery took far longer than the loading itself.

Ordinary daily life does this too. The Achilles takes about [3,500 ± 1,700 load cycles a day](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/id/eprint/18425), and its diameter swings between −25% and +10% over 24 hours.

**What this means:** a tendon tested or loaded hard in the late afternoon after a busy day is not in the same state as in the morning. Standardize timing when you measure tendon thickness or stiffness.

### 4.3 Fatigue: when loading comes too often

Fatigue in tendon is cumulative micro-damage plus a lag in repair. In one study, 12 consecutive days of high-strain loading [raised maximal Achilles strain by about 20%](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/download/4c4c5342f33a0a84390ee3361b0b8b9b1c52aff78609c51654e6721e14c220c4/305155/Epro%2C%20Suhr%20%26%20Karamanidis_JExpBiol_R1_Revised_paper_FINAL.pdf), even though tendon force rose about 10%. The authors concluded that frequent high cyclic loading over a short period may lower the tendon's tolerance to high load. A softer tendon under the same force is working closer to failure.

**Practical fatigue markers (no lab needed):**

1. **Morning stiffness** of the tendon (minutes to settle). A rise is an early warning.
2. **Load-test pain:** a standard single-leg decline squat (patellar) or 10 single-leg hops or heel raises (Achilles), rated 0–10. Rate it each morning.
3. **24-hour response:** pain or stiffness that is worse the next morning means the previous day's load exceeded current capacity.
4. **Performance markers:** a falling countermovement jump or reactive strength index with rising tendon pain points to tendon fatigue rather than muscle fatigue.

These markers have face validity and are used in RCT protocols (Ch. 6), but their predictive validity is not established (grade D).

### 4.4 Response rate: responders and non-responders

Tendon rehab has a large non-responder group. Chronic Achilles tendinopathy has [a relatively high percentage of non-responders](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6356409) to standard eccentric training. Responses to isometrics are [variable both within and across tendinopathy populations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7406028/). In the Breda trial, return to sport at 24 weeks was [43% with progressive loading versus 27% with eccentric training](https://cm.healio.com/news/orthopedics/20211029/progressive-tendonloading-yields-positive-outcomes-in-patients-with-patellar-tendinopathy). More than half of patients were still not back to sport at 6 months.

Common reasons for non-response (grade D):

- the dose was too low to strain the tendon (body-weight-only programs);
- the load flared the tendon and was never adjusted;
- compression at an insertional site was not removed;
- kinetic-chain deficits were not addressed (calf for patellar, hip for gluteal);
- metabolic or medication factors (Ch. 9);
- follow-up was too short; gains in Breda appeared only at 24 weeks.

### 4.5 Deterioration: what happens with rest or immobilization

| Duration | Muscle | Tendon | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 days of suspension | Synthesis down | Collagen synthesis [roughly halved (0.052 → 0.023 %/h)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2375459) | B |
| 14 days | Size \~5% down | Tendon size and turnover [largely unchanged](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/short-term-immobilization-and-recovery-affect-skeletal-muscle-but) | B |
| 21–23 days | Size \~10% down | Synthesis down \~80%; size still stable | B |
| \~7 weeks (cast) | Calf CSA and strength down | [Achilles CSA unchanged](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/effects-of-long-term-immobilization-and-recovery-on-human-triceps) | B |

Tendon structure is resistant to short-term rest, but its *synthesis* drops quickly. After 2 weeks of immobilization, the tendon's [acute collagen response to a run was still normal](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-012-2450-5). The main danger after a layoff is that muscle force returns before tendon capacity, which widens the mismatch from section 3.6.

### 4.6 Load spikes

A spike is a sudden rise in tendon-specific load above what the tendon has recently handled. Typical triggers include the start of preseason, more jump sessions, a surface change, a new strength block, extra matches, or a return after a break.

In young elite volleyball players, [every extra training hour per week raised the odds of jumper's knee 1.72-fold](https://www.iat.uni-leipzig.de/datenbanken/iks/ta/Record/4030037), and match exposure was the strongest sport-related predictor. Chapter 7 covers how to measure spikes and how good the metrics really are.

**Go deeper:** Wearing et al. 2013 (BJSM) on transverse strain recovery; Magnusson et al. 2010 on the net-loss window.

## Chapter 5 — Training modes compared

**No contraction type is magic. Tendons respond to strain magnitude, time under strain and progression.** Eccentric, heavy slow resistance (HSR), isometric and progressive programs give similar clinical results when each is dosed well. Choose by irritability, adherence, stage and the sport's demands.

### 5.1 Summary table

| Mode | What it is | Best use | Key evidence | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Isometric | Static holds, e.g. 5 × 30–45 s at a high % MVC, or 3-s holds | Highly irritable tendon; in-season maintenance; stiffness work (3-s holds) | Not better than isotonic for pain ([Clifford 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32818059/)); 3-s high-strain holds raised stiffness 57% ([Bohm 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25267851/)) | A/B |
| Heavy slow resistance (HSR) | Concentric + eccentric, \~3 s each way, 15RM progressing to 6RM, 3x/week | First-line strength phase for patellar and Achilles | Patellar: best satisfaction at 6 months ([Kongsgaard 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19793213/)); Achilles: equal to eccentric with [100% vs 80% satisfaction at 12 weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018970/) | A |
| Eccentric | Lowering only (Alfredson heel drops; decline squats), often twice daily | Mid-portion Achilles; when HSR equipment is unavailable | Effective but no better than HSR; higher time burden | A |
| Progressive staged loading (PTLE) | Isometric → isotonic → plyometric → sport, progressed by pain | Patellar tendinopathy in athletes | Beat eccentric by 9 VISA-P points at 24 weeks ([Breda 2021](https://cdn.fs.pathlms.com/516KLedMQu6PrdC0LeIa)) | A |
| Plyometric / energy-storage | Hops, bounds, jumps; fast stretch–shortening | Late rehab; restores spring function | Only a trend in stiffness from jumps ([Bohm 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25267851/)); needed for return to sport | B/D |
| Running and sport loading | Graded exposure to the actual task | Return to play | Continued running under a pain rule [showed no negative effects](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389) in Achilles tendinopathy | A/B |

### 5.2 Isometrics: what they can and cannot do

Isometrics became popular after small studies reported immediate pain relief. Larger trials and reviews since then are mixed. A 2020 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found isometrics [not superior to isotonic exercise, either immediately or over 12 weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32818059/). A quasi-randomized Achilles trial found [no significant pain reduction on a hop test](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7496962/) after any isometric or isotonic protocol. A patellar network meta-analysis found isometric and isotonic exercise [equally effective for immediate pain relief](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8634001).

**Use isometrics for:** (1) a starting load when anything dynamic flares the tendon; (2) a low-fatigue in-season stimulus; (3) **short 3-s high-strain holds** to build stiffness. Do not rely on them as a pain "switch".

### 5.3 HSR vs eccentric: why HSR is now often preferred

Both work. HSR needs only 3 sessions a week, against about twice daily for classic eccentric programs. It is easier to progress with a load you can measure. In the patellar trial, [HSR also increased collagen network turnover](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/corticosteroid-injections-eccentric-decline-squat-training-and-he/), while corticosteroid gave good short-term but poor long-term results.

**A standard HSR template** (from the Copenhagen trials; gym-based):

| Weeks | Load | Sets × reps | Tempo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 15RM | 3 × 15 | 3 s up / 3 s down |
| 2–3 | 12RM | 3 × 12 | 3/3 |
| 4–5 | 10RM | 4 × 10 | 3/3 |
| 6–8 | 8RM | 4 × 8 | 3/3 |
| 9–12 | 6RM | 4 × 6 | 3/3 |

The template is usually built from 3 bilateral exercises (patellar: squat, leg press, hack squat; Achilles: seated and standing calf raise, leg press calf raise). Allow pain up to about 5/10 that settles by the next morning. Rest 2–3 min between sets.

### 5.4 Plyometrics and speed: the missing phase

Strength alone does not restore the spring. Basketball needs the patellar and Achilles tendons to absorb and return energy at high rates. Without a staged plyometric phase, athletes often flare when they return to sport. The Breda protocol added plyometrics and running [every third day](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/progressive-tendon-loading-vs-eccentric-exercise), only after the isometric and isotonic stages were tolerated. Spacing high-strain plyometrics 48–72 h apart is consistent with the collagen timeline in Chapter 3.

### 5.5 Kinetic chain and compression

Two modifiers apply to every mode:

- **Compression:** insertional Achilles, proximal hamstring and gluteal tendons are squeezed against bone at end range. Early on, avoid deep dorsiflexion (insertional Achilles), deep hip flexion (hamstring) and hip adduction (gluteal). Reintroduce these ranges later.
- **Kinetic chain:** calf capacity affects patellar load, and hip strength affects knee loading. Train the whole chain, not just the painful tendon.

**Go deeper:** Malliaras et al. on loading programs; Silbernagel's Achilles program; Breda 2021 supplementary protocol.

## Chapter 6 — Dose and progression

**Progress tendon load by criteria, not by calendar: the gate is the tendon's 24-hour response, not the date.** Use three tools together: dose variables (what to change), the pain-monitoring model (how much discomfort is acceptable), and stage gates (when to move on).

### 6.1 The dose variables

| Variable | Evidence-based target | Notes | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strain magnitude | High: \~70–90% MVC, aiming for \~4.5–6.5% tendon strain | The [Arampatzis series](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-56840-6.pdf) used 0.17 Hz loading at 4.5–6.5% strain with \~3 s duration | A/B |
| Time under strain per rep | \~3 s | 12-s holds gave smaller gains than 3-s holds | B |
| Frequency (cycles) | Low, \~0.17 Hz (3 s on / 3 s off) | Fast jumps alone add little stiffness | B |
| Sets per session | \~4–5 | About 10 min of tendon work | B/C |
| Sessions per week | 3–4 heavy | 48–72 h between heavy sessions | B |
| Block length | >12 weeks for structure | Pain improves sooner than structure | A |
| Contraction type | Any | Choose by tolerance | A |

### 6.2 The pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel)

Silbernagel's RCT tested whether athletes with Achilles tendinopathy could keep running and jumping during rehab. Patients in the training group were allowed [pain up to 5/10 during activity](https://www.sportsmd.com/sports-injuries/hip-thigh-injuries/achilles-tendinopathy). Pain after exercise could [reach 5 but had to settle by the next morning](https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/continued-sports-activity-using-a-pain-monitoring-model-during-rehabilitation-in-patients-with-achilles-tendinopathy/), and pain and stiffness were not allowed to increase from week to week. The trial [found no negative effects](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/18389) from continuing tendon-loading activity under these rules.

| Zone (0–10) | During activity | Next morning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Safe | 0–2 | Back to baseline | Progress |
| Acceptable | 3–5 | Back to baseline | Hold the load |
| High risk | >5, or rising | Worse than baseline | Reduce load for 1–3 days, then retest |
| Trend rule | — | Rising week to week | Reduce weekly volume |

### 6.3 The continuum model (Cook & Purdam)

The continuum model proposes [three states of tendon structure: reactive tendinopathy, tendon disrepair and degenerative tendinopathy](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/handle/2299/11773). Each maps to a different clinical picture and management focus.

| Stage | Typical patient | What the tendon is doing | Load strategy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reactive | Young athlete after a load spike; acute swelling | Cell activation, proteoglycan swelling; reversible | Cut provocative load (jumps, compression); isometrics; keep the athlete training elsewhere |
| Disrepair | Recurrent flares | More matrix disorganization, some neovessels | Progressive strength (HSR) plus graded energy storage |
| Degenerative | Older athlete or long history | Areas of disordered matrix; limited reversibility | Build capacity in the healthy tendon around it; long-term HSR |
| Reactive-on-degenerative | Older athlete with an acute flare | Flare in a tendon with existing changes | Settle the flare, then build capacity |

The model is useful but not proven. Its authors note that [classifying tendinopathy by structure, in what is mainly a pain condition, has been challenged](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5118437). Use it to guide load choices, not as a diagnosis (grade D).

### 6.4 Stage gates for return to sport

The stages follow the Breda PTLE structure and the Silbernagel rules. The gates are clinical criteria (grade D unless stated).

1. **Stage 1 — Settle and isometric.** Daily isometrics (e.g. 5 × 30–45 s). Remove compression and spikes.
   - *Gate:* load-test pain ≤3/10 and stable over 1 week.
2. **Stage 2 — Strength (HSR or isotonic).** 3x/week, progressing 15RM → 6RM. Breda alternated [isometrics and isotonics on separate days](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/progressive-tendon-loading-vs-eccentric-exercise).
   - *Gate:* single-leg strength about 80–90% of the other side; 25 single-leg heel raises (Achilles) or a pain-free single-leg squat to 60° (patellar).
3. **Stage 3 — Energy storage.** Plyometrics and running every 2–3 days, building from double-leg to single-leg and from low to high volume.
   - *Gate:* hop and jump tests at about 90% of the other side, with no next-day flare.
4. **Stage 4 — Sport.** Graded court or field exposure: technical work, then controlled practice, then full practice, then competition.
   - *Gate:* full practice for 1–2 weeks with stable 24-hour response; VISA score trending up.
5. **Maintenance.** In season, keep 1–2 heavy sessions a week. Strength drops off quickly when it stops.

### 6.5 How fast to progress

- Change **one variable at a time**: load, then volume, then speed.
- A common rule is about a 10–20% weekly increase in tendon-specific load (jumps, sprints, heavy sets). This is a heuristic, not tested (D).
- After any flare, step back one level for 1–3 days. Do not go back to complete rest.

**Go deeper:** Cook & Purdam 2009 (BJSM); Cook et al. 2016 "Revisiting the continuum model" (BJSM); Silbernagel et al. 2007 (AJSM).

## Chapter 7 — Load spikes and injury prediction

**No single test or load metric predicts which individual athlete will develop tendinopathy.** Several factors raise group-level risk: imaging abnormalities, training and match volume, male sex in jumping sports, and high jumping ability. Use them to decide *who to monitor closely*, not *who will get injured*.

### 7.2 The "talent trap"

The athletes most at risk are often the best jumpers. Lian and colleagues proposed that risk is [especially high for young players moving from junior to senior elite level](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/17/1265.full). Better jumpers produce higher impact loads. When strength, muscle mass and training load also rise quickly, the tendon load rises faster than the tendon can adapt (the mismatch from Ch. 3). **Practical meaning:** extra tendon conditioning and closer monitoring are warranted for promoted youth and newly drafted players.

### 7.3 The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR)

**The idea.** ACWR divides recent load (usually the last 7 days) by longer-term load (usually the [mean of the previous 3–6 weeks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569)). It was derived from Banister's fitness–fatigue model. Athletes with [ACWR ≥1.5 were described as at higher risk](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569) of acute and overuse injury.

**The critique.** A series of analyses by Impellizzeri and colleagues concluded there is [no evidence supporting ACWR for training-load management or injury-reduction advice](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/citation/handle/10453/141898). They showed that ratio artefacts and reclassifications [inflate the apparent link](https://www.springermedizin.de/what-role-do-chronic-workloads-play-in-the-acute-to-chronic-work/18697072), and recommended dismissing ACWR as a framework. Gabbett's own later review still holds that [high chronic load, aerobic fitness and lower-body strength](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534940) protect against load spikes.

**What survives both sides (grade B/D):**

1. Sudden large rises in sport load are a plausible risk. Avoid them, especially for tendon-specific load such as jumps and sprints.
2. A high, well-built chronic load protects better than keeping load low.
3. Strength is protective, so keep strength work going in season.
4. Do not use a ratio cut-off (e.g. 1.5) as an injury alarm or to make individual decisions.

### 7.4 Measuring tendon-relevant load

General load (session RPE × minutes, GPS distance) misses what tendons feel. Better tendon-specific inputs:

- **Jump count and jump height:** from IMUs or video. Basketball players differ a lot in jump volume even within the same team. Within-squad differences in jump frequency were reported in youth volleyball ([Bahr & Bahr abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/7/564.1.full.pdf)).
- **High-speed running and accelerations/decelerations:** Achilles and patellar load.
- **Heavy-lift exposure:** sets above about 80% 1RM for knee-dominant and calf lifts.
- **Match minutes and back-to-back games.**

### 7.5 Screening: what it can and cannot do

Ultrasound screening finds athletes with raised *group* risk. Most athletes with abnormal scans will still never develop symptoms, and some with normal scans will. [Other factors beyond tissue structure](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/50/24/1516.full.pdf) clearly contribute.

The practical value of screening is to **flag athletes for closer monitoring and preventive loading**, not to keep anyone out of sport. An abnormal pain-free tendon is not an injury.

**Go deeper:** McAuliffe et al. 2016 (BJSM); Impellizzeri et al. 2020–2021 (IJSPP, Sports Med); Visnes & Bahr 2013 (SJMSS).

## Chapter 8 — Assessment toolkit

**Assess tendons on three separate axes: pain and function (questionnaires), capacity (strength and load tests), and structure (imaging).** They often disagree, so never let one stand in for the others. Structure guides risk; pain and capacity guide rehab decisions.

### 8.1 Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)

| Tool | Region | Format | Measurement properties | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VISA-P | Patellar | 8 items, 0–100 (100 = normal) | Original test-retest stable; [Dutch version ICC 0.74](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2734752); French version [ICC 0.99](https://researchportal.unamur.be/files/87949659/Kaux_-_VISA-P_VISA_A.pdf) | Content validity evidence rated [very low quality](https://www.physiotutors.com/questionnaires/visa-p-questionnaire/) |
| VISA-A | Achilles | 8 items, 0–100 | [Test-retest r=0.93; intra- and inter-rater r=0.90](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1724384) | Most used outcome in Achilles RCTs |
| VISA-G | Gluteal | 8 items, 0–100 | Validated (Fearon 2015, from memory) | Lateral hip |
| VISA-H | Proximal hamstring | 8 items | Validated (Cacchio 2014, from memory) |  |
| PRTEE | Lateral elbow | 15 items, pain + function | Widely validated (from memory) |  |
| WORC / SPADI | Rotator cuff / shoulder | Multi-domain | Widely validated (from memory) |  |

**Interpreting change.** A VISA change of about 13 points or more is often treated as clinically important (from memory, grade D). Healthy athletes usually score 95–100. In the French validation, mean VISA-P was [99 in healthy people, 53 in patients and 86 in at-risk athletes](https://researchportal.unamur.be/files/87949659/Kaux_-_VISA-P_VISA_A.pdf).

### 8.2 Capacity and provocation tests

Reliability for these tests is generally good (ICC >0.8) when protocols are standardized. Their validity as return-to-sport gates is weaker (grade D).

| Test | Tendon | Method | Reference value / gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single-leg heel raise to fatigue | Achilles | Metronome 30 bpm, full range, count reps and total work | Healthy adults \~25+ reps; aim for limb symmetry ≥90% |
| Single-leg decline squat (25° board) | Patellar | Rate pain 0–10 at a fixed depth | Daily morning tracking; pain ≤3/10 to progress |
| Single-leg hop (10 reps) | Achilles/patellar | Rate pain; contact time | Stable or falling pain week to week |
| Knee extension or plantarflexion MVC (dynamometer) | Patellar/Achilles | Isometric, fixed angle | Limb symmetry ≥90% |
| CMJ and drop jump (force plate) | Whole chain | Jump height, RSI, landing asymmetry | Back to baseline; asymmetry <10–15% |
| Hip abduction MVC (side-lying) | Gluteal | Handheld dynamometer | Symmetry; compare to body mass norms |

### 8.3 Imaging

| Method | What it shows | Use | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grey-scale ultrasound | Thickness, hypoechoic areas, fibre disruption | Screening; diagnosis; risk (≥4x risk if abnormal) | Operator-dependent; abnormality ≠ pain |
| Power/colour Doppler | Neovessels | Supports diagnosis; [present in all 12 symptomatic tendons](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1725221) in one junior volleyball study | Affected by activity before the scan; probe pressure |
| Ultrasound tissue characterization (UTC) | 3-D fibre-pattern stability | Research; tracking of load response | Specialist equipment |
| Shear-wave elastography | Local stiffness estimate | Research; side-to-side comparison | Values depend on depth, angle and tension |
| MRI | Thickening, signal, partial tears, bone oedema | Surgery planning; unclear cases; tears | Cost; abnormal findings common in pain-free athletes |

**Timing rule:** tendon thickness drops by up to about 15% after heavy exercise and recovers over 6–24 h (Ch. 4). Scan at the same time of day, after at least 24 h without heavy tendon loading, with the joint in a standard position.

### 8.4 A minimum assessment battery

1. **Baseline (preseason):** VISA for at-risk tendons; ultrasound of the patellar and Achilles tendons; CMJ; single-leg heel raise; isometric knee extension and plantarflexion.
2. **Weekly in season:** morning load-test pain (decline squat, hop); tendon stiffness rating; jump count and minutes.
3. **Every 4–6 weeks:** strength and CMJ retest; VISA repeat for anyone with pain.
4. **When symptomatic:** add Doppler ultrasound, a load-test series and a review of the kinetic chain.

**Go deeper:** Robinson et al. 2001 (VISA-A); Visentini et al. 1998 (VISA-P); Docking & Cook on UTC.

## Chapter 9 — Tendon health essentials

**Loading is the main treatment, but whole-body factors set how well a tendon responds.** Metabolic health, some medications, hormones and nutrition can double or halve risk. Screen for them in every tendon patient.

### 9.1 Systemic risk factors

| Factor | Effect | Evidence | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diabetes | Tendinopathy [more prevalent in people with diabetes (OR 3.67)](https://bjsm.bmj.com/lookup/ijlink/YTozOntzOjQ6InBhdGgiO3M6MTQ6Ii9sb29rdXAvaWpsaW5rIjtzOjU6InF1ZXJ5IjthOjQ6e3M6ODoibGlua1R5cGUiO3M6NDoiQUJTVCI7czoxMToiam91cm5hbENvZGUiO3M6ODoiYmpzcG9ydHMiO3M6NToicmVzaWQiO3M6OToiNTAvMTYvOTgyIjtzOjQ6ImF0b20iO3M6MjI6Ii9ianNwb3J0cy81MS8yLzk3LmF0b20iO31zOjg6ImZyYWdtZW50IjtzOjA6IiI7fQ==); mechanism is AGE crosslinking | A | Ask about glucose; slower progression; longer blocks |
| Fluoroquinolone antibiotics | Higher Achilles rupture risk, [especially in the first month (OR 1.1–7.1)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23888427/); worse with age >60 | B | Ask about recent antibiotics; pause high-load and plyometric work during the course and for some weeks after |
| Fluoroquinolone + corticosteroid | Excess risk of any tendon rupture [21.2 per 10,000 person-years](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30682225/) vs 3.73 for fluoroquinolones alone | B | Highest-risk combination |
| Corticosteroid injection | Good short-term, poor long-term effects in patellar tendinopathy ([Kongsgaard 2009](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/corticosteroid-injections-eccentric-decline-squat-training-and-he/)) | A | Avoid as first-line in athletes; never as the only treatment |
| Age | Smaller acute tendon response with age ([Wearing](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/48/5/383.full.pdf)); core collagen not renewed | B | Longer blocks; more recovery between heavy sessions |
| Oral contraceptives (high estrogen) | Smaller exercise-induced rise in collagen synthesis | B | Note it; no evidence for changing medication for tendon reasons |
| Alcohol | [A possible risk factor](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35937777/) for Achilles tendinopathy and rotator cuff tears; findings inconsistent | B/C | Moderate intake |
| Obesity, high cholesterol, smoking | Associated with tendinopathy and tears (from memory) | B/C | Address in the general health plan |

### 9.2 Nutrition

A 2022 systematic review found that [combining training with dietary supplements seemed to give better clinical and functional results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35937777/). Collagen-derived peptides were the most-studied supplement. The evidence base is small, and several trials are industry-funded.

| Strategy | Dose used in trials | Evidence | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adequate energy intake | Avoid low energy availability | Low energy availability is linked to poor connective-tissue health (from memory) | B/D |
| Protein | \~1.6 g/kg/day for athletes in heavy training | Supports muscle; indirect for tendon | B |
| Specific collagen peptides + training (healthy men) | \~5 g/day for 14 weeks | Greater Achilles CSA gain [+11.0% vs +4.7%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35403756/); no stiffness difference | B |
| Collagen peptides + calf program (tendinopathy) | Daily for 3 months, crossover | [May speed the benefits](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4LeKm2jl) of a calf-strengthening and return-to-run program; pilot; [funded by GELITA](https://pureportal.bcu.ac.uk/en/publications/oral-supplementation-of-specific-collagen-peptides-combined-with--2/) | C/B |
| Gelatin + vitamin C before loading | 15 g gelatin + 50 mg vitamin C, 30–60 min before 6 min of rope skipping | Raised a collagen synthesis marker in blood (Shaw et al. 2017, from memory) | B (marker only) |

**Practical protocol (grade C/B):** 10–15 g gelatin or collagen peptides plus about 50 mg vitamin C, 30–60 min before a tendon session. It is low-risk and cheap, but it is an add-on to loading, not a replacement.

### 9.3 Recovery behaviours

- **Sleep:** essential for general recovery. There is no tendon-specific RCT (D).
- **NSAIDs:** may blunt the collagen response to training in young people (mixed evidence, from memory). In older adults during 2-week immobilization, NSAIDs [had no influence on tendon collagen synthesis](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/tendon-collagen-synthesis-declines-with-immobilization-in-elderly/). Avoid routine use around tendon-building blocks.
- **Heat, ice, massage:** may help comfort, but have no proven tendon remodelling effect (D).

### 9.4 Red flags and referral

Refer for imaging or medical review if any of these are present:

- sudden pop with weakness (suspect rupture; check Thompson test for Achilles);
- pain at rest or at night, or systemic symptoms;
- bilateral or multi-site tendon pain (consider inflammatory disease such as spondyloarthritis, or high cholesterol);
- tendon pain soon after fluoroquinolone use;
- no improvement after 12 weeks of well-dosed loading.

**Go deeper:** Ranger et al. 2016 (BJSM); IOC consensus on dietary supplements (2018); Morales et al. 2019 (Br J Clin Pharmacol).

## Chapter 10 — Body-wide tendon map

**The same principles apply everywhere, but each tendon has its own load profile, compression position and best evidence.** The two tendons with the strongest RCT evidence are the Achilles and patellar. Gluteal and lateral elbow have good trials. Most other regions rely on extrapolation (grade D).

### 10.1 Region-by-region table

| Tendon | Typical population | Provocation test | Compression to avoid early | First-line loading | Best evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Achilles mid-portion | Runners, court sports, 30–60 yrs | Single-leg heel raise, hop | Little | HSR or eccentric; keep running under pain rule | Beyer 2015; Silbernagel 2007 (A) |
| Achilles insertional | Older runners, Haglund shape | Heel raise off a step (pain at the insertion) | Deep dorsiflexion off a step | Heel raises on flat ground; heel lift; progress range later | Smaller trials (B/D) |

| Quadriceps | Older jumpers, lifters | Squat, kneel | Deep flexion | As patellar | Extrapolated (D) |
| Proximal hamstring | Sprinters, distance runners | Bent-knee bridge, deep hip flexion stretch | Sitting, deep hip flexion | Isometric bridges → hip-dominant HSR → sprint | Small trials (B/D) |
| Gluteal (lateral hip) | Women 40–70, runners | Single-leg stance 30 s; FADER | Hip adduction (crossing legs, side-sleeping) | Education + progressive hip abductor loading | Mellor 2018 LEAP (A) |
| Adductor longus | Football, ice hockey | Squeeze test | Wide stretch | Copenhagen adduction progression | Prevention RCTs (A/B, from memory) |
| Rotator cuff | Overhead athletes, 40+ | Painful arc; resisted external rotation | End-range abduction under load | Progressive cuff and scapular loading | Several RCTs (B) |

| Medial elbow | Golf; throwing | Resisted wrist flexion | — | Wrist flexor loading | Extrapolated (D) |
| Tibialis posterior | Women 40+, flat foot | Single-leg heel raise (arch stays flat) | — | Orthosis + tibialis posterior loading | Small RCTs (B) |
| Peroneal | Ankle instability, runners | Resisted eversion | — | Eversion loading; balance | Extrapolated (D) |

### 10.2 Achilles (court athletes)

The Achilles carries the highest absolute loads in the body (Ch. 2). Mid-portion pain sits 2–6 cm above the heel. Insertional pain is at the bone and is worsened by compression in dorsiflexion.

HSR and eccentric programs are equally effective. HSR showed [100% vs 80% satisfaction at 12 weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26018970/), but this difference was gone by 52 weeks. Athletes can keep running and jumping under the 5/10 pain rule. For rupture prevention, watch for fluoroquinolones, steroid injections and sudden returns after a layoff.

### 10.3 Patellar (jumping athletes)

Prevalence is [44.6% in elite volleyball and 31.9% in basketball](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15722279/). Pain is usually at the inferior pole of the patella, in the deep posterior fibres.

The best-supported program is staged progressive loading (Breda). Expect results at about 24 weeks, not 12. HSR is a strong alternative. Avoid corticosteroid injections in athletes. During a flare, cut jump volume first and keep strength work going.

### 10.4 Gluteal tendinopathy (lateral hip)

In the LEAP trial, 204 participants with MRI-confirmed gluteal tendinopathy were randomized to [education plus exercise (14 sessions over 8 weeks), a corticosteroid injection, or wait and see](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29720374/). Education plus exercise beat wait-and-see, with a [number needed to treat of 2.0](https://www.ndorms.ox.ac.uk/publication/926873). It also gave better global improvement than the injection.

The key element is **load management education**: avoid crossing legs, "hanging" on one hip while standing, and lying on the painful side. Combine this with progressive abductor strength work.

### 10.5 Upper-limb tendons

The principles transfer: settle irritability, load progressively, then restore speed and endurance. Positional tendons have lower in-use strain, so endurance and motor-control work matter more. Lateral elbow tendinopathy responds poorly in the long term to corticosteroid injection; this is well established in a JAMA RCT (Coombes 2013, from memory).

**Go deeper:** Mellor et al. 2018 (BMJ); Beyer et al. 2015 (AJSM); the Grimaldi lateral hip model; Littlewood on rotator cuff loading.

## Chapter 11 — Practical playbook

**Run tendon care as a weekly system: monitor every morning, load heavy 2–3 times a week, space high-strain sessions 48–72 h apart, and change one variable at a time.** The templates below are built for basketball and other jumping sports. They combine the evidence in Chapters 3–7 with clinical rules (grade D where noted).

### 11.1 Monitoring system

| Frequency | Measure | Time needed | Trigger for action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily (morning) | Load-test pain 0–10 (decline squat / hop); morning stiffness | 1 min | Pain up by ≥2 points, or worse than the day before |
| Every session | Jump count, minutes, heavy sets | Automatic or 1 min | Weekly tendon load >20% above the 3-week average |
| Weekly | VISA for symptomatic players; CMJ | 5 min | VISA down ≥10 points; CMJ down >5% with pain |
| Every 4–6 weeks | Isometric strength; heel raise to fatigue; ultrasound if indicated | 15 min | Limb asymmetry >10%; new US abnormality → closer monitoring |

### 11.2 Decision rules

1. **Pain ≤3/10 and next morning normal** → continue the plan; progress one variable next week.
2. **Pain 4–5/10, settles by morning** → hold this week's load; do not progress.
3. **Pain >5/10, or worse the next morning** → cut jump and sprint volume 30–50% for 2–3 days, keep or switch to isometrics, keep upper-body and non-provocative training, then retest.
4. **Rising trend over 2 weeks** → cut weekly tendon load 20–30%, check sleep, schedule, medications and recent spikes.
5. **Sudden pain with weakness or a pop** → stop and refer for imaging.

### 11.3 In-season weekly template (one game a week)

| Day | Court | Tendon/strength work | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Game day (G) | Game | Morning: 5 × 30 s isometric knee extension / calf (optional) | Low-fatigue prep |
| G+1 | Recovery | Mobility; upper body | No high-strain tendon work |
| G+2 | Practice (moderate) | **Heavy session:** HSR 3–4 × 6–8 squat or leg press, calf raises | Main tendon stimulus |
| G+3 | Practice (high) | Plyometrics within practice; no extra jumps | Track jump count |
| G+4 | Practice (moderate) | **Second heavy session** (lower volume) + 3-s high-strain holds | 48 h after the first |
| G–1 | Walk-through | Short isometrics only | Taper |

**Two games a week:** keep one heavy session (the day after the first game's recovery day), plus isometrics. Do not drop strength work entirely for more than 10–14 days.

### 11.4 Preseason ramp (6 weeks)

| Week | Court and jump load | Strength | Tendon focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | \~50% of target practice volume | HSR 3 × 12–15 | Establish morning monitoring and baselines |
| 2 | \~60% | 3 × 10–12 | 3-s holds at high strain |
| 3 | \~70% | 4 × 8–10 | Introduce plyometric blocks every 48–72 h |
| 4 | \~80% | 4 × 6–8 | Single-leg plyometrics |
| 5 | \~90% | 4 × 6 | Full practice intensity |
| 6 | 100% | Maintain | Game simulation |

The \~10–20% weekly increase is a clinical rule (D). The real gate is a stable 24-hour response (Ch. 6).

### 11.5 Return to sport after tendinopathy: checklist

- [ ] Load-test pain ≤2/10 for 2 weeks
- [ ] Strength symmetry ≥90% (isometric or RM)
- [ ] Hop and jump tests ≥90% of the other side, with no next-day flare
- [ ] Has completed full practice for 1–2 weeks
- [ ] VISA ≥80, or back to the player's pre-injury level
- [ ] Has an in-season maintenance plan (1–2 heavy sessions a week)

### 11.6 Special groups

| Group | Main risk | Change to the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adolescents in a growth spurt | Muscle–tendon mismatch; insertional pain (Osgood–Schlatter, Sever's) | Cap jump volume; add 3-s high-strain holds; monitor growth rate |
| Promoted youth and rookies | Talent trap: sudden jump in level and volume | Extra preseason ramp; weekly VISA |
| Women | Lower collagen synthesis; ACL risk | Longer strength blocks; neuromuscular ACL program |
| Athletes returning from a layoff | Muscle returns faster than tendon | Rebuild tendon capacity for 2–4 weeks before full jump load |
| Masters athletes (35+) | Slower response; rupture risk | Fewer high-strain sessions a week; longer warm-up; medication review |

## Chapter 12 — Same-day loading, timing and reset

**A tendon loaded again before it has recovered is softer, partly dehydrated, biologically less responsive and in its breakdown phase.** It gives more risk for less adaptation. The rule that follows: one high-strain tendon session per day, ideally in the morning, with the second session of the day low-strain, and 48–72 h before the next heavy session.

### 12.1 The adaptation timeline: seconds to months

| Time scale | What changes in the tendon | Practical meaning | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seconds (each rep) | Viscoelastic response: stiffer when loaded fast | Fast landings feel "stiffer"; a 3-s hold reaches full strain | A |
| First few reps / minutes | Preconditioning: repeated loading lowers stiffness and hysteresis | A warm-up changes the tendon briefly | B |
| \~10 min | Cell signalling peaks and switches off | Extra loading after \~10 min adds wear more than signal | C |
| 0–6 h | Fluid squeezed out; thickness drops up to \~15%; cells refractory | A second session in this window loads a thinner, "switched-off" tendon | B (fluid) / C (cells) |
| 6–24 h | Thickness recovers (fast in the first 6 h, complete by \~24 h) | The tendon is mechanically "reset" by the next morning | B |
| 24–72 h | Breakdown first, then synthesis peaks (\~24 h) and stays high (\~72 h) | The biological reset; schedule heavy sessions on this rhythm | B |
| 2–3 weeks | Synthesis falls quickly with unloading | Layoffs lower the tendon's building rate fast | B |
| 8–14 weeks | Stiffness, modulus and CSA change | Real capacity gains | A |
| Years | Core collagen essentially fixed after adolescence | Adult capacity comes from material quality and the periphery | B |

### 12.2 Loading an unrecovered tendon in the afternoon

Picture a player who did heavy squats and plyometrics in the morning and then has a high-jump practice at 5 pm. Four things are different about the tendon at 5 pm:

1. **Less fluid, thinner tendon.** After heavy work, thickness dropped about 15% and was still recovering for up to 24 h ([Grigg/Wearing series](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3102919)). The tissue is mechanically different from the morning.
2. **Lower stiffness.** In one study, repeated contractions [significantly decreased stiffness](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsair__edsair.doi...........f565d077ab68e51044097659e91435bd). A softer tendon strains more at the same force, so it works closer to its failure strain (Ch. 2).
3. **Cells less responsive.** In engineered ligament, a second bout within 6 h produced little new signal (C). The extra session adds wear with little adaptation.
4. **Breakdown phase.** The first \~24 h after heavy loading is when net collagen loss can occur (Ch. 3).

The accumulated effect over days is real in humans: 12 consecutive days of high-strain loading [raised maximal tendon strain by \~20%](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/download/4c4c5342f33a0a84390ee3361b0b8b9b1c52aff78609c51654e6721e14c220c4/305155/Epro%2C%20Suhr%20%26%20Karamanidis_JExpBiol_R1_Revised_paper_FINAL.pdf), a sign of reduced tolerance.

**How large is the risk?** No trial has measured injury rates from same-day double high-strain sessions (D). The mechanisms all point the same way, and the player-level data on extra training hours agree (OR 1.72 per extra hour; Ch. 7).

### 12.3 Double-session rules

| Situation | Rule | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two sessions in one day | Put the high-strain tendon work in **one** session (usually the morning). The other session is low-strain: technique, upper body, conditioning without jumps | D |
| Two high-strain sessions unavoidable (camps, tournaments) | ≥6 h apart; second session lower in volume (≤50%) and intensity; monitor next-morning pain | C/D |
| Heavy strength + court practice on the same day | Strength first (fresh tendon, best quality), then court ≥6 h later, or court first and strength the next day | D |
| Back-to-back game days | Isometrics only between games; no extra plyometrics | D |
| Heavy session followed by | 48–72 h before the next high-strain tendon session | B |

### 12.4 The reset idea

A **mechanical reset** happens overnight (fluid and thickness). A **biological reset** takes 48–72 h (synthesis catches up with breakdown). A **symptom reset** is confirmed only when next-morning load-test pain is back to baseline.

Use the morning load test as the reset check:

- **Back to baseline:** the reset is complete. Progress as planned.
- **Up by 1–2 points:** partial reset. Make the day low-strain.
- **Up by ≥3 points:** no reset. Take 48–72 h of reduced tendon load (isometrics allowed), then retest.

### 12.5 Warm-up: does it make loading safer?

Warm-up changes the tendon, but briefly and mostly through muscle.

| Warm-up type | Effect on tendon | Duration of effect | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 min static stretching | Stiffness [22.9 → 20.6 N/mm; hysteresis 20.6 → 13.5%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11160050/) | Short | Kubo 2001 (B) |
| Short static stretching (5 × 60 s) | Changes were [muscle, not tendon](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6816483); muscle stiffness drop lasted \~5 min | Minutes | B |
| Repeated contractions | Stiffness down; hysteresis unchanged ([Kubo 2002](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsair__edsair.doi...........f565d077ab68e51044097659e91435bd)) | Short | B |
| 3 weeks of daily stretching | Stiffness unchanged; [hysteresis fell from 19.9 to 12.5%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11796669/) | Chronic | B |
| Resistance training (12 weeks) | Stiffness [+18.8%; with stretching added +15.3%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2290007) | Chronic | B |

**Practical warm-up (D):** 8–12 min of progressive dynamic work, such as easy running, then 2–3 sets of submaximal tendon-specific loading (bilateral heel raises, half squats), then low-amplitude hops building to full jumps. Keep long static stretching for after sessions or separate sessions. No study shows that a warm-up prevents tendinopathy. Its main value is preparing muscle and neural drive, so the tendon is loaded in a controlled way.

### 12.6 Extra loading after practice

Players often add hundreds of extra jumps or shots after practice. The tendon gets little extra signal after the first \~10 min (C) but keeps accumulating cycles and fatigue. **Rule (D):** extra post-practice work should be low-impact, such as form shooting without jumping, free throws and footwork. Count any extra jumps in the day's tendon load.

### 12.7 Building forceful activity safely: the force-and-speed ladder

Progress one rung at a time. Move up only when the next-morning response is stable for about 1 week.

1. Isometric, high load, 3-s holds
2. Slow heavy isotonic (HSR, 3 s up / 3 s down)
3. Faster concentric, controlled lowering (power lifts at 40–60% 1RM)
4. Low-amplitude bilateral hops and skipping
5. Bilateral jumps and landings, increasing height
6. Single-leg hops, bounds and depth landings
7. Sport-specific reactive tasks: cuts, rebounds, repeated jumping

A useful check (D) is whether the athlete can do the current rung at the planned volume twice in one week with a stable morning response. Only then move up.

**Go deeper:** Kubo et al. 2001–2002 (J Appl Physiol; Acta Physiol Scand); Wearing et al. 2013 (BJSM); Paxton 2012 (Tissue Eng A).

## Chapter 13 — The support network: how other structures help or hurt the tendon

**A tendon never works alone.** The load it sees is set by the muscle pulling on it, the joints around it, the fascia it blends into, and the nervous system that times every contraction. Many "tendon" problems are failures somewhere else in this network. Many tendon solutions are improvements somewhere else too.

### 13.1 The network

| Structure | How it changes tendon load | Training lever | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Muscle (same unit) | Stronger muscle = more force on the tendon; fatigued muscle = poorer control, more tendon strain per landing | Build strength *with* tendon stiffness, not ahead of it (Ch. 3) | B |
| Aponeurosis | Shares stretch with the free tendon; stores energy | Same high-strain loading | B/C |
| Neighbouring muscles (kinetic chain) | Redistribute work between hip, knee and ankle in landing | Hip and calf strength to share knee load | D |
| Fascia (crural, fascia lata/ITB, plantar) | Links muscles in series; transmits some force sideways | Whole-chain loading; foot strength for the plantar fascia | C/D |
| Bone and enthesis | Insertion shape sets compression | Avoid end-range compression early (Ch. 5) | B/D |
| Joint mechanics | Landing knee angle, foot strike and trunk lean change tendon moment arms | Landing and running technique | B/D |
| Nervous system | Sets recruitment, timing and rate; pain changes motor control | Externally paced strength training; skill practice | B/C |

### 13.2 Muscle as the tendon's "driver"

The muscle decides how much force reaches the tendon and how fast. A strong, fresh muscle controls a landing smoothly. A fatigued muscle lands stiffer or less controlled. Both raise peak tendon strain.

The mismatch problem (Ch. 3) runs in both directions. If muscle strength outpaces tendon stiffness, [operating strain rises and the safety factor falls](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7332733). If muscle is weak or inhibited, the tendon also loses its stimulus and stays under-loaded.

**Practical rule (D):** test both muscle strength (MVC) and tendon behaviour (strain on ultrasound, or at least symptom response) during a strength block. Increase plyometric load only when both are rising together.

### 13.3 Kinetic chain sharing

In a landing, the hip, knee and ankle share the work of absorbing force. If the calf is weak, more work shifts to the knee and the patellar tendon. If the hip is weak, the knee and trunk compensate.

- **Patellar tendon:** train the calf and the hip extensors as well as the quadriceps.
- **Achilles:** train the soleus at high loads (seated calf raise), not just the gastrocnemius.
- **Gluteal tendons:** train trunk and pelvic control, and cut loads in hip adduction.

These are clinical reasoning and biomechanical models rather than trial findings (D).

### 13.4 Fascia

Fascia links muscles in series and spreads some force sideways into neighbouring tissues. The plantar fascia works with the Achilles–calf complex in push-off; the fascia lata and ITB sit next to the gluteal tendons. Direct evidence that fascia "offloads" tendons in humans is thin (C/D). The practical takeaway is to load the whole chain and avoid treating the tendon in isolation.

### 13.5 The nervous system: learning, unlearning and relearning

Tendon pain changes how the brain drives the muscle. Rio and colleagues reviewed [changes to the primary motor cortex and motor control in tendinopathy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4752665). People with patellar tendinopathy showed altered corticospinal excitability and inhibition of the quadriceps ([Rio thesis](https://bridges.monash.edu/articles/thesis/Corticospinal_responses_to_patellar_tendon_pain_and_the_effects_of_externally_paced_strength_training/4697392)). They argue that rehab aimed only at the tendon [is unlikely to address these corticospinal and neuromuscular adaptations](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/4/209.full).

Their proposed solution is **tendon neuroplastic training (TNT)**. Strength training that is [externally paced and similar to a skilled movement task](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4752665) has been shown to reduce tendon pain and change excitatory and inhibitory control of the muscle.

**How to apply TNT (C/D):**

- Use a metronome or audio/visual cue: e.g. 3 s up and 4 s down, set to a beep.
- Ask for focused attention on the movement; no distractions or phones.
- Use the same loads as HSR or isometric programs; pacing is the extra ingredient.
- Add skill: target depths, single-leg control, then sport-like decision tasks.

The evidence is mechanistic plus small trials and case reports ([elbow case series](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6173218)). It is low-cost and easy to add.

### 13.6 The learning loop

Tendon rehab works as a loop that runs at three speeds:

1. **Tissue loop (weeks to months):** load → collagen and crosslink remodelling → higher capacity → more load.
2. **Motor loop (days to weeks):** paced practice → better recruitment and landing control → smoother tendon loading → less pain.
3. **Perception loop (sessions to weeks):** successful loading → less fear and threat → more confident movement → more load accepted.

Each loop can stall. Fear can block the motor loop. A painful flare can reverse the perception loop. Under-dosing stalls the tissue loop. Good rehab moves all three forward together. **Relearning** after injury means restoring all three, not only tendon structure.

**Go deeper:** Rio et al. 2016, "Tendon neuroplastic training" (BJSM); Mersmann et al. on muscle–tendon imbalance; Lepley on eccentric neural effects.

## Chapter 14 — Tears and healing

**A torn tendon heals with scar that is weaker, less organized and often longer than the original tendon.** It never fully returns to normal. The two jobs of rehab after a tear are to protect length early and load progressively late. In the Achilles, millimetres of elongation decide long-term function.

### 14.1 Healing phases

| Phase | Timing | What happens | Loading focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inflammatory | [\~24–72 h](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8721391/table/table1-23259671211062929), up to \~7 days | Clot, inflammatory cells, growth-factor release; [ECM synthesis begins](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10588330/table/iwj14261-tbl-0002) | Protect; pain-guided movement; avoid stretching the gap |
| Proliferative (repair) | [Few days to \~6 weeks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11580759/table/T3) | Fibroblasts multiply; rapid type III collagen; new vessels | Gentle, protected loading; isometrics; early weight-bearing in a boot (Achilles) |
| Remodelling: consolidation | [\~6–10 weeks](https://www.scribd.com/document/539530728/Basic-tendon-healing) | Cellularity and synthesis fall; fibres begin to align | Progressive resistance |
| Remodelling: maturation | \~10 weeks to a year or more | [Type I replaces type III; collagen aligns and crosslinks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10588330/table/iwj14261-tbl-0002) | Heavy loading, then energy storage and sport |

In sheathed tendons (fingers), healing from inside the tendon (intrinsic) gives better gliding. Healing from surrounding tissue (extrinsic) produces [adhesions and a biomechanically compromised tendon](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11580759/table/T3). Early controlled motion favours intrinsic healing.

**The ceiling:** even after a good recovery, [the tendon–muscle unit may not return to normal even after 10 years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10905747).

### 14.2 Essential components of healing

| Component | Why it matters | How to provide it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Controlled mechanical load | Aligns new collagen; drives type III → type I | Graded loading from the proliferative phase on |
| Protection of length | Gap and elongation reduce function permanently | Achilles: protected plantarflexion early; avoid stretching into dorsiflexion for \~8–12 weeks |
| Blood supply | Delivers cells and nutrients | Active movement; avoid smoking (D) |
| Inflammation (early) | Starts repair | Avoid routine anti-inflammatories in the first days ([PEACE](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/2/72.full)) |
| Nutrition | Raw material for collagen | Adequate energy and protein; collagen/vitamin C add-on (Ch. 9) |
| Time | Remodelling takes months | Return-to-sport gates by criteria (Ch. 6) |
| Systemic health | Diabetes, age and medications slow healing | Screen and manage (Ch. 9) |

### 14.3 Achilles rupture: the millimetre problem

**Operative vs nonoperative.** A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,628 participants) found much lower re-rupture with surgery: [RR 0.28 for minimally invasive and 0.30 for open repair](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12624965). Open surgery had more complications (RR 3.03). The BMJ 2019 meta-analysis found re-rupture of [2.3% after surgery](https://advances.massgeneral.org/ortho/journal.aspx?id=1260). But with an **accelerated functional rehabilitation** program, [there was no significant difference in re-rupture](https://advances.massgeneral.org/ortho/journal.aspx?id=1260) between surgery and nonoperative care. The main extra complication of surgery was [infection (2.8%)](https://www.pubmed.gov/30617123/).

**Elongation.** [Regardless of surgical or non-surgical treatment, the injured tendon elongates during healing](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00167-022-07142-9). Elongation happens mostly in the first 6 months and [is negatively correlated with heel-rise function](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11022787). In one cohort, [elongation of more than 3 cm significantly reduced jumping symmetry](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/achilles-tendon-cross-sectional-area-at-12weeks-post-rupture-rela/). Tendon CSA at 12 weeks was the strongest predictor of heel-rise height at 1 year (R² = 0.28).

**Measuring length in clinic.** The Achilles tendon resting angle (ATRA), measured with the patient prone and knee bent, [relates to tendon elongation within the first year](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6047896). At 12 months, absolute ATRA [correlated with heel-rise height (r = −0.63)](https://doaj.org/article/480233613db64255b085fb4aace5e023). It is a free, fast way to track lengthening.

| Achilles rupture metric | How to measure | Target / warning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ATRA (relative to the other side) | Goniometer, prone, knee 90° | Loss of plantarflexion angle = elongation warning |
| Tendon length | Extended-field-of-view ultrasound | Side-to-side difference; >3 cm linked to poor jumping |
| CSA at 12 weeks | Ultrasound | Larger CSA predicts better heel rise |
| Heel-rise height | Single-leg heel raise | Limb symmetry index; >30% deficit at 1 year links to worse long-term ankle mechanics ([Brorsson](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/heel-rise-height-deficit-1-year-after-achilles-tendon-rupture-rel/)) |
| Heel-rise work and reps | Metronome test | ≥90% symmetry for return to sport |

### 14.4 Rotator cuff tears: size in centimetres

| Size class (DeOrio & Cofield) | Dimension | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small | [≤1 cm](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2325967121998791) | Best healing after repair |
| Medium | >1 to <3 cm | Good healing |
| Large | ≥3 to <5 cm | Higher retear |
| Massive | ≥5 cm | Retear reported [40–94%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5613358) |

Across repairs, retear rates are often [7–17%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7745616), or about [20%](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11999-017-5251-7). Supraspinatus fat over 33.2% [predicted retear better than Goutallier grades](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12059232). Many patients still improve functionally even with a retear.

### 14.5 Partial tears and tendinopathy-related tears

Partial tears (patellar, proximal hamstring, gluteal, cuff) usually start in tendinopathic tissue. Most are managed with the same progressive loading approach, adjusted for irritability. Surgery is considered for large, symptomatic tears that do not respond, or for acute avulsions such as a proximal hamstring avulsion with retraction (grade D).

**Go deeper:** Ochen et al. 2019 (BMJ); Willits et al. 2010 (JBJS); Silbernagel et al. 2012 (AJSM) on elongation; Zellers et al. on ATRA.

## Chapter 15 — Immediate solutions, treatments and technology

**There is no instant fix that rebuilds a tendon.** What can change quickly is pain and load. Remove the provoking load, keep the tendon loaded within tolerance, and add treatments only where evidence shows they add something to exercise. A large network meta-analysis found [no convincing evidence that any adjunct, alone or with exercise, beats exercise alone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/10409676/).

### 15.1 The first 72 hours after an acute injury: PEACE & LOVE

PEACE & LOVE replaced RICE and POLICE because the older acronyms [ignored the subacute and chronic stages of healing](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/2019/04/26/soft-tissue-injuries-simply-need-peace-love/).

| Letter | Meaning | Detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P**rotect | Unload for 1–3 days | [Restrict movement for 1–3 days](https://www.proquest.com/docview/2348245071) to limit bleeding; keep rest short, since long rest weakens tissue |
| **E**levate | Limb above the heart | Reduces swelling |
| **A**void anti-inflammatories | No routine NSAIDs or ice early | They [may harm optimal tissue repair](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/2/72.full) |
| **C**ompress | Bandage or tape | Reduces swelling |
| **E**ducate | Explain the process | Avoid unnecessary passive treatment and imaging |
| **L**oad | Pain-guided return to activity | Start as symptoms allow |
| **O**ptimism | Confident expectations | Linked to better outcomes |
| **V**ascularisation | Pain-free cardio | Improves blood flow and mood |
| **E**xercise | Restore strength, mobility and proprioception | Main long-term driver |

### 15.2 Quick wins for a painful (intact) tendon

These change symptoms within days. None of them change tendon structure.

| Option | When to use | Evidence | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cut the provoking load (jumps, sprints, compression positions) by 30–50% | Any flare | Principle behind all RCT protocols | B |
| Isometric holds (e.g. 5 × 30–45 s) | Highly irritable tendon; before training | No better than isotonics for pain on average; individual responses vary | A |
| Heel lift | Insertional Achilles | Reduces dorsiflexion compression | D |
| Avoid compression postures | Gluteal (no leg crossing), hamstring (limit deep sitting), insertional Achilles | LEAP trial education component | A/B |
| Taping or patellar strap | Short-term comfort | Weak | D |
| Keep training elsewhere | Upper body, cycling, pool | Keeps fitness without tendon load | D |

### 15.3 Treatments compared

| Treatment | Best current evidence | Verdict | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Progressive exercise | Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses | First line for every tendinopathy | A |
| Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) | 13-RCT meta-analysis: [not superior to placebo at 4–6, 12 or ≥24 weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34342296/). For patellar tendinopathy after failed conservative care, [multiple injections appeared better than eccentric exercise](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10893258) | Second-line at most; mixed evidence | A/B |
| Shockwave (ESWT) | Adding ESWT to eccentric training in patellar tendinopathy gave [no short-term benefit (moderate evidence)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/10409676/). Reviews suggest it may be [similar to surgery long term](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10893258/) | Option for resistant cases; not a replacement for loading | B |
| Corticosteroid injection | Good short-term, poor long-term in patellar; worse than education + exercise in gluteal | Avoid in athletes; caution near the Achilles | A |
| High-volume injection, GTN patches, sclerosing injections | Small trials; inconsistent | Experimental | C/D |
| Surgery (debridement, tenotomy) | For persistent cases after 6–12 months of good rehab | Last resort for tendinopathy; standard for some tears | B/D |

## Chapter 16 — Measuring the tendon: the closed feedback loop

**You cannot measure tendon force directly in daily practice, but you can chain five measurable layers to estimate it.** Those layers are external load, estimated internal load, tissue response, symptoms and performance. Measure all five and feed them back into the next day's plan. That turns tendon care from guessing into a closed loop.

### 16.1 The five layers

| Layer | What it captures | Example measures | Validity | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. External load | What the athlete did | Jump count, landing height, sprint distance, sets × reps × kg, minutes | Good for counting; weak for tendon force | Low |
| 2. Estimated internal load | What the tendon felt | Tendon force from moment arms; musculoskeletal models; force-plate impulse | Moderate; models need individual data | Medium–high |
| 3. Tissue response | How the tendon changed | Thickness (US), stiffness (SWE, dynamometry + US), Doppler, UTC | Good in the lab; time-of-day effects | Medium–high |
| 4. Symptoms | How the tendon "reports" | Morning load-test pain, stiffness duration, VISA | Good reliability; central to RCT protocols | Very low |
| 5. Performance | What the tendon allows | CMJ, RSI, heel-rise work, hop symmetry | Good reliability | Low–medium |

### 16.2 Estimating internal tendon force by hand

A tendon's force is the joint moment divided by its moment arm. For a single-leg heel raise:

```latex
F_{tendon} = \frac{F_{ground} \times d_{external}}{d_{tendon}}
```

With body weight 800 N, an external moment arm (ball of foot to ankle) of 0.15 m and an Achilles moment arm of 0.05 m, the force is 800 × 0.15 / 0.05 = 2,400 N, or about 3 times body weight. Running reaches roughly 6–12 times body weight (Ch. 2). This simple calculation shows why body-weight calf raises cannot prepare a tendon for running, and why loaded calf work is needed. The moment arms here are typical textbook values; individual values vary (D).

### 16.3 Biomarkers

Collagen turnover markers (PINP for synthesis; ICTP and CTX-I for breakdown) track the tendon response well when sampled **next to** the tendon by microdialysis. Langberg's runners showed [synthesis and breakdown markers falling after a run, then rebounding over 72 h](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2269656). Blood levels mostly reflect bone and whole-body turnover, so they cannot monitor a single tendon (B/D). For now, biomarkers are research tools, not field tools.

### 16.4 The daily closed loop

1. **Plan:** set today's tendon load (jumps, heavy sets, sprints) from yesterday's data.
2. **Load:** record what was actually done (layer 1).
3. **Respond:** next morning, record load-test pain, stiffness and, weekly, CMJ (layers 4–5).
4. **Compare:** has the response stayed within the athlete's normal range?
5. **Adjust:** progress, hold or cut load using the rules in Ch. 11.
6. **Learn:** over weeks, find each athlete's personal dose–response, i.e. the load that consistently gives a stable next-morning response.

### 16.5 A minimum data record (per athlete per day)

| Field | Unit | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jump count and estimated height | Count, cm | IMU or video |
| High-speed running | m | GPS or estimate |
| Heavy sets (knee-dominant, calf) | Sets × reps × kg | Gym log |
| Session RPE × minutes | Arbitrary units | Athlete report |
| Morning load-test pain | 0–10 | Athlete report |
| Morning stiffness | Minutes | Athlete report |
| CMJ (weekly) | cm, RSI | Force plate or app |
| VISA (if symptomatic) | 0–100 | Questionnaire |
| Context | Sleep, illness, medications, travel | Athlete report |

With this record, the athlete's own history becomes the reference. No population threshold such as ACWR is needed.

## Chapter 17 — Brainstorm: ideas, hypotheses and concepts

**These are untested ideas built on the evidence in this booklet.** Each says what it would test and how to test it cheaply. Treat every item as grade D until data exist.

### 17.1 Training ideas

| Idea | Rationale | How to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"Morning tendon, afternoon skill"** day structure | High-strain work on a fully reset tendon; low-strain skill on a tendon in its fluid-recovery window | Compare 8 weeks of morning vs afternoon heavy sessions in two matched squads; track morning pain and CMJ |
| **Micro-dosed 3-s holds** (e.g. 5 × 3 s, 2–3 times a day, ≥6 h apart) | Tests whether the in-vitro 10-min/6-h rule transfers to humans | Crossover trial: one daily session vs split micro-doses; tendon stiffness by US at 12 weeks |
| **Tendon-first preseason** | 4 weeks of high-strain tendon work before jump volume rises | Compare tendon pain incidence across seasons with and without the block |
| **Metronome everything** | Adds the neural (TNT) effect to every strength session for free | Randomize paced vs self-paced HSR; VISA and quadriceps inhibition |
| **Mismatch-guided plyometrics** | Plyometric volume rises only when tendon stiffness tracks strength gains | Monthly strength + tendon strain testing in youth |

### 17.2 Measurement ideas

| Idea | Rationale | How to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Personal tendon "reset score"** | Combine morning pain, stiffness minutes and CMJ change into one 0–100 daily score | Check whether score drops precede flares by 2–5 days in a season cohort |
| **Jump-weighted load** | Weight each jump by estimated height and landing type (single vs double leg) | Compare to raw jump count as a flare predictor |
| **Phone-video ATRA** | Measure Achilles resting angle from a photo after rupture | Agreement study against a goniometer |
| **Time-stamped ultrasound** | Always scan at the same time, ≥24 h after heavy load, to remove fluid effects | Test–retest reliability with vs without the protocol |
| **Tendon dose–response map** | Learn each athlete's load band that gives a stable next-day response | N-of-1 designs inside a monitoring platform |

### 17.3 Health and recovery ideas

- **Collagen + vitamin C timing study** in basketball players, with jump-heavy practices as the loading stimulus and VISA plus US as outcomes.
- **Antibiotic and steroid alert:** link medical records to the training plan, so high-strain sessions are automatically reduced during fluoroquinolone courses.
- **Glucose-aware loading** for athletes with diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance: longer blocks and more recovery days.

### 17.4 The integrated loop concept

An ideal tendon system would link five components:

1. **Sense:** wearables for jumps and sprints, a short daily symptom check, a weekly jump test.
2. **Estimate:** convert external load into estimated tendon load using each athlete's body size and moment arms.
3. **Compare:** detect deviations from the athlete's own baseline, not from population cut-offs.
4. **Decide:** apply the rules from Chapters 6, 11 and 12 to suggest progress, hold or reduce.
5. **Learn:** update each athlete's dose–response profile every week, and publish anonymized cohort findings.

### 17.5 Biggest open bets

1. Human proof (or disproof) of the 10-min / 6-h timing rules.
2. A validated tendon-specific load metric that beats raw training hours.
3. A field-friendly stiffness measure reliable enough to steer weekly loading.
4. Response-predictor profiles that identify likely non-responders before rehab starts.

## Chapter 18 — Myths and open questions

**Much of what is taught about tendons comes from cell studies, small trials or expert opinion.** The myths below are common in practice. The open questions are where new research, or a well-built monitoring platform, could add real knowledge.

### 12.1 Myths vs evidence

| Myth | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| "Tendinopathy is inflammation; rest and anti-inflammatories fix it" | It is a failed-healing, load-related condition. Rest lowers synthesis and capacity; steroid injection relapses long term |
| "Eccentrics are the only proven treatment" | HSR, progressive staged loading and eccentrics all work; HSR is easier to keep up |
| "Isometrics switch off tendon pain" | Meta-analyses show no superiority over isotonic exercise; responses vary |
| "Plyometrics make tendons stiffer" | Jumps alone produced only a trend; high-strain 3-s holds were far better |
| "Heavy lifting makes tendons compliant and slow" | Heavy, high-strain loading raises stiffness and modulus |
| "An abnormal scan means the athlete is injured" | It raises group risk about 4-fold; many abnormal tendons stay pain-free |
| "ACWR >1.5 predicts injury" | Methodological critiques find no reliable support for the ratio |
| "A 10-minute window and 6-hour gap are proven in humans" | Shown in engineered ligaments only |
| "Collagen supplements rebuild tendon" | Small, partly industry-funded trials show modest CSA or symptom benefits; loading is still the driver |

### 12.2 Open questions

1. **Dose–response in athletes.** What minimum weekly high-strain dose maintains tendon stiffness in season?
2. **Individual response.** Why do some patients fail to respond? Candidates include genetics, pain processing, metabolic state, and under-dosing.
3. **Spike thresholds.** Is there a tendon-specific load rise (jumps, sprints) that reliably precedes flares? This would need large datasets with daily tendon pain and jump counts.
4. **Translating the cell-culture timing rules.** Does a 6-hour gap between short bouts beat one longer session in humans?
5. **Sex and hormones.** Do women need different block lengths or loading frequency? No RCT has tested this.
6. **Imaging as a biomarker.** Can UTC or shear-wave elastography track adaptation week to week reliably enough to guide load?
7. **Muscle–tendon imbalance screening.** Can routine strength and tendon-strain testing in youth identify who needs tendon-specific conditioning?

### 12.3 Research designs that would help

- **N-of-1 and cohort designs** with daily morning load-test pain, jump counts and weekly strength: cheap, and they test spike thresholds directly.
- **Pragmatic RCTs in season** comparing 1 vs 2 heavy sessions a week for tendon maintenance.
- **Dose-titrated trials** that set load by measured tendon strain (ultrasound), not by % 1RM, following the Arampatzis approach.

**Go deeper:** Cook et al. 2016 "Revisiting the continuum model"; Impellizzeri et al. 2020 on causal frameworks for load–injury research.
